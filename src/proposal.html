Executive summary

You already started this. Let us finish it where it counts.

Constantine Engineering Associates recently refreshed its homepage, and the new page works. It has a clear hero, an organized services grid across Fire Protection, Plumbing, Mechanical, and Electrical, a featured project strip, and clean calls to action. That investment was the right instinct. The issue is that the refresh stopped at the front door.

The moment a visitor clicks into the portfolio — which, for an engineering firm, is the single most important section — the site drops back to the older template. A polished homepage leads into thirteen separate, text-heavy portfolio pages that are hard to scan, impossible to filter, and largely invisible to search engines. The strongest material CEA owns, including the historic HVAC project on King Street where an entire system was concealed inside twelve chimney flues, is buried in paragraphs and parked on the testimonials page.

Sandbar Digital proposes carrying the quality of the new homepage through the rest of the site and rebuilding the portfolio into a filterable, case-study-driven experience that is fast, mobile-friendly, and built to rank. The three sections that follow address the priorities CEA named, in order.

Ease of navigation

What is happening now

  • The main menu lists the entire sitemap twice, and the Portfolio dropdown alone holds thirteen market links: Commercial, Ecclesiastical, Educational, Government, Health Care, Historical, Hospitality, Industrial, Mixed-Use, Municipal, Recreational, Residential, and Senior Living.
  • A visitor has to know which category their building falls under before they can find relevant work. Someone who simply needs an electrical engineer has no way to browse by service.
  • Each market lives on its own long single-column page with no way to filter, sort, or cross-reference. Finding a comparable project means scrolling and guessing.
  • The experience between the new homepage and the older interior pages is inconsistent, which makes the brand read as two different companies the moment someone clicks in.

What Sandbar would do

ChangeWhat it does for the visitor
One Portfolio link The thirteen portfolio links collapse into a single destination, keeping the top-level menu to roughly five clear choices. Current UX standards recommend short, uncluttered primary navigation: a shorter menu is faster to scan and far easier on a phone.
On-page filtering Deep menu hunting is replaced by filters. Visitors land on one portfolio and narrow it themselves by market, by service, and by location. The work comes to them instead of the other way around.
Breadcrumbs and a back path Every project page shows where the visitor is and offers a predictable way back, without reaching for the browser button.
One primary action per page Each page carries a single clear call to action — Start Your Project — with secondary links kept visually subordinate, so the next step is never ambiguous.
A rebuilt mobile menu A clean accordion with comfortable tap targets, since a large share of this traffic arrives on a phone.
Design standards applied in this section Navigation kept short and uncluttered  ·  predictable back behavior and deep linking to filtered views  ·  one primary call to action per screen  ·  touch targets of at least 44 × 44 pixels with comfortable spacing on mobile.

Project and case study work

This is where the largest gain is, and where an engineering firm actually wins or loses the decision. The information CEA has is genuinely strong. It is the presentation that is holding it back.

What is happening now

  • Every project is one image plus a dense, fully italicized paragraph. Long italic text is hard to read, and the structure changes from one entry to the next, so nothing is scannable.
  • Imagery is inconsistent. Some projects use real photography, others low-resolution screenshots or renderings, and the image shapes vary widely, so the gallery never feels intentional.
  • There are no individual project pages. Everything lives on one scroll per market, leaving no room to tell the story of a project, show before and after, or pair a photograph with a drawing.
  • The facts buyers care about — square footage, services provided, the architect, and CEA's role — are trapped inside sentences instead of presented as clean, scannable specifications.
  • The best story on the entire site, the King Street historic HVAC project concealed inside twelve chimney flues with only six new nail holes, sits on the testimonials page, where almost no prospect will connect it to the firm's capability.

What this costs CEA today

More than one hundred completed projects, thirteen market pages, and the firm's single best engineering story are all on the site right now. A prospect evaluating CEA on a phone can scan almost none of it, cannot filter it, and will not find it in search. The work is there; the presentation is what is losing the decision.

Design standards applied in this section A consistent style across all pages  ·  readable body text with comfortable line height instead of long italic blocks  ·  uniform image aspect ratios with reserved space to prevent layout shift  ·  shared element transitions from card to detail view  ·  visual hierarchy built through size, spacing, and contrast.

What Sandbar would build

ComponentWhat it is
One filterable portfolio A single grid replaces the thirteen separate pages. Visitors filter by market, by service (Fire Protection, Plumbing, Mechanical, Electrical), and by location, and can combine filters — Hospitality plus Electrical in Charleston, for example. Each filtered view gets its own link, which is good for sharing and good for search.
A standardized project card Every project uses the same card: one uniformly cropped image, the project name, the location, a single-line descriptor, small service badges for the disciplines CEA provided, and the square footage. Consistent cards make the whole body of work feel organized and serious, and they read cleanly on a phone.
Dedicated case study pages Ten to fifteen of the strongest projects get their own page: a hero image, a specifications sidebar (location, size, market, services, architect, year, CEA's role), the engineering narrative, and a small gallery. King Street is the first one built — a challenge that specific, and that well solved, is exactly the proof that moves an architect or owner from browsing to calling.
Social proof beside the work CEA has excellent testimonials, including fifteen-year client relationships and award-winning historic restorations. Those quotes belong next to the projects they describe, not isolated on a separate page, so credibility lands at the moment a prospect is evaluating the work.

Search engine optimization

The portfolio pages should be the firm's best magnets for searches like Charleston hospitality MEP engineer. Today they are the least optimized pages on the site — a large and recoverable opportunity.

What is happening now

  • Interior portfolio pages ship with blank meta descriptions, so search engines have nothing curated to show and the firm gives up control of its own search snippet.
  • Image alt text is largely missing or simply repeats the caption, which costs both accessibility and image search visibility.
  • There is no structured data on the site, so search engines are not being told that CEA is a local business with two offices, or that these pages describe specific projects.
  • Images are heavy and unoptimized, which slows the pages. Page speed and layout stability are confirmed ranking and experience factors.
  • Because all projects sit on thirteen long pages with no individual URLs, there are far fewer indexable, keyword-focused pages than the firm's real body of work could support.

What Sandbar would do

WorkWhy it matters
Metadata on every page A unique, keyword-aware title and meta description leading with the market, the service, and the city, so each page competes for the searches that matter.
Descriptive alt text Every meaningful image described, which helps screen readers, image search, and overall page relevance at once.
LocalBusiness structured data Charleston and Lexington both marked up with consistent name, address, phone, and hours, to strengthen local and map visibility.
Project structured data Added to each new case study page so search engines understand the content and the pages become eligible for richer results.
Image and speed work Modern formats, compression, lazy loading below the fold, and declared dimensions so the layout does not shift as the page loads.
More indexable pages Flagship projects become individual pages, multiplying the number of focused, rankable URLs and strengthening internal linking throughout the site.
Standards applied in this section Descriptive alt text on meaningful images  ·  sequential heading hierarchy  ·  modern image formats with lazy loading and declared dimensions for layout stability  ·  color contrast of at least 4.5 : 1 for readable, accessible text.

Recommended approach

There are two honest ways to deliver this. The line that separates a tidy-up from a real improvement is the filterable, case-study-driven portfolio. Sandbar recommends the rebuild.

  Improve in place Rebuild Recommended
Navigation Trim the menu and add light tag-based filtering within current template limits. A single filterable portfolio, true multi-filter, and a clean mobile menu.
Case studies Tidy the existing pages and use more consistent images. Standardized cards plus dedicated project pages with full specifications and the story.
Search Fix metadata and alt text on the existing pages. Full metadata, structured data, speed work, and many new indexable pages.
Speed Limited by the current platform. A fast static build, optimized media, and strong Core Web Vitals.
Best for A quick cleanup on a tight budget. Making the portfolio actually drive new business.

How the rebuild runs

PhaseFocusDeliverables
1  Discovery Confirm goals, gather project assets, select flagship case studies. Project list, asset checklist, sitemap, and filter taxonomy.
